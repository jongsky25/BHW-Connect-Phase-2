## Glossary of terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Assessment** | A systematic process for gathering, assessing and documenting information to assign a level of risk for a potential public health event. Enables objective evidence-based decisions while giving consideration to the uncertainties and limitations of the information available at a particular point in time. |
| **Barangay Health Worker (BHW)** | A person who has undergone training programs under any accredited government or non-government organization and who voluntarily renders primary health care services in the community after having been accredited to function by the Local Health Board (LHB), in accordance with DOH guidelines. |
| **Community-Based Surveillance (CBS)** | Or community event-based surveillance: the detection and reporting of unusual health events or health risks occurring within a community, by community members including community volunteers, community health or animal health workers, the public, religious leaders, civil society members, teachers, and other similar groups. |
| **Confirmation** | The first step taken after a signal is reported, in which the public health worker ensures that reported information is accurate and true. |
| **Event** | Per the International Health Regulations (IHR): "a manifestation of disease or an occurrence that creates a potential for disease" — infectious, zoonotic, food safety, chemical, radiological or nuclear in origin, whether transmitted by persons, vectors, animals, goods/food or through the environment. |
| **Event-Based Surveillance (EBS)** | The organized and rapid capture of information about events that are a potential risk to public health, including events related to the occurrence of a disease in humans and events related to potential risk-exposures in humans. Can be rumors or ad-hoc reports through formal channels (routine reporting systems) or informal channels (media, health workers, NGO reports). |
| **Event-Based Surveillance and Response (ESR)** | The program developed by the DOH to increase the level of alertness to emerging public health threats and respond to them quickly. |
| **Public Health Risk** | Per IHR: "a likelihood of an event that may affect adversely the health of human populations, with an emphasis on one which may spread internationally or may present a serious and direct danger." |
| **Response** | Action and decisions carried out immediately, including verification or field investigation of health events through coordination with local health authorities and other involved institutions. Adapted according to the nature of the public health risk. |
| **Reporting** | The process by which health events and health risks are brought to the knowledge of the health authorities. |
| **Risk** | The likelihood of an event resulting in negative consequences for public health. |
| **Signal** | Data and/or information considered by the Early Warning and Response System as representing potential acute health risk, such as an outbreak. May consist of reports of cases or deaths (individual or aggregated), or potential exposure of human beings to animal, environment, biological, chemical, or radiological and nuclear hazards. Can be detected through any potential source (health or non-health, informal or official) including the media. |
| **Verification** | The process by which the health event report is substantiated. Includes confirming the basic information about time, place and person. |

## 1. Introduction

All countries are required by international law to establish systems capable of detecting,
reporting and responding to significant public health threats. Community involvement is crucial.
While healthcare providers sometimes spot outbreaks, they might not always catch them early enough
to stop them. This guideline describes an early warning system involving the community, focusing on
the Barangay Health Station (BHS).

## 2. Purpose of the guideline

To help BHWs, midwives, and nurses set up and improve Community-Based Surveillance. Includes how to
involve key community members and what important events to report to the Municipal or City
Epidemiology and Surveillance Units (MESU/CESU).

## 3. Overview of the disease surveillance system in the Philippines

The Philippine Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (PIDSR) monitors diseases in several
ways. Some systems collect regular disease reports from healthcare facilities to track diseases like
influenza and dengue.

Community-Based Surveillance is different: **it looks for new outbreaks of rare but serious
diseases.** Reports from the community, or information from news or social media about unusual
sickness, help identify these threats. BHWs are in the best position to hear and check these reports
because they live and work in the community.

## 4. Priority diseases for community-based surveillance

Diseases that spread quickly, cause severe illness or death, or can be controlled early with
vaccines:

- Malaria
- Vaccine-preventable diseases: measles, whooping cough, rubella, diphtheria, polio
- Diseases from animals: rabies, anthrax, leptospirosis, bird flu
- Diseases from contaminated food and water: cholera, typhoid
- New diseases that may appear or come from outside the community, e.g. from a returning traveler

## 5. Roles of key participants

CBS is managed at the local level by the MESU and CESU, who collect, organize, and analyze health
information. The DSO leads these efforts.

**Disease Surveillance Officer (DSO):**
- Oversee health surveillance in their area, including at Barangay Health Stations.
- Train BHWs in community-based surveillance.
- Investigate reported health events and cases, including contact tracing.
- Report urgent health events and diseases to higher authorities quickly.
- Work with local agriculture, environment, and education offices on health events related to
  animals or the environment.
- Assess the risk and impact of reported health events.

At the barangay level the midwife or nurse at the BHS handles many DSO duties, trained by the DSO.

**Nurse / Midwife:**
- Receive and quickly forward reports from BHWs to the DSO.
- Verify events reported by BHWs.
- Support, train, and give feedback to BHWs on surveillance.
- Advise the community on how to protect themselves from local health threats, working with BHWs.

**Barangay Health Worker (BHW)** — crucial to the CBS team, responsible for detecting and reporting
unusual health events:
- Identify and stay in contact with Key Informants (KIs) in the community who can report health
  threats.
- Check signals reported by Key Informants based on DOH guidelines.
- Report signals to the nurse or midwife supervisor, or directly to the LESU if no supervisor is
  present.
- Provide feedback to Key Informants after verifying signals.
- Advise the community on health protection, working with the nurse/midwife.
- Help verify, assess risk, investigate, and respond to health events as needed.

The Barangay Animal Health Worker, farmers, livestock inspectors and forest rangers are critical
community key informants for animal and environment signals.

## 6. Key concepts

### 6.1 Principles of CBS

The goal is to quickly detect and respond to public health threats. Community members are best
positioned to notice unusual events and should be trained on what to look for and how to report.

> To shorten the time between detecting a threat and responding to it, it is important to report any
> suspicion of a health threat, **even before knowing the exact cause**. Recognizing a signal means
> noticing a pattern of illness that suggests something might be wrong, without needing to know the
> specific disease causing it.

*Figure: Finding outbreaks when they are small will prevent more cases.* — 1 sick person → 2 sick
people reported by a local pastor to the BHW → BHW takes action; versus sick people not noticed
until large numbers appear at hospital.

### 6.2 Signals

Signals are signs that an important public health event, such as an outbreak, might be happening in
the community. Signals can include:

- Unexpected deaths in the community (one person or a group)
- Groups of people with similar illnesses
- Reports of individuals with a high-priority disease, like measles
- Groups of animals suddenly getting very sick or dying, like a flock of chickens or shorebirds

When a signal is detected it should be reported to BHWs immediately. The BHW confirms the details of
the report, including the timeframe, to determine if the signal is real. Confirmed signals are
recorded in the BHS logbook and reported to the MESU/CESU through the nurse/midwife supervising the
BHS.

### 6.3 Sources of signals

Signals can come from many sources: community members (especially Key Informants), healthcare
providers, social media, or changes in routine surveillance data.

**Key Informants** are well-connected individuals who might recognize that something unusual is
happening among their daily contacts. A teacher or school headmaster would notice an unusual number
of children absent because of illness. A local pastor or Imam might recognize when an unusual number
of congregants are sick at the same time.

Key Informants could include:

- Community and political leaders
- Religious leaders
- Teachers or school headmasters
- Leaders of social groups, clubs, or unions
- Pharmacists
- Hairdressers or other service providers
- Market overseers and traders
- Farmers and forestry workers
- Youth association leaders

**In cities**, people may be more connected through social groups than through neighborhoods. A city
resident may be much more aware of someone they attend church with than of someone in the same
apartment building. Urban Key Informants might therefore also be drawn from:

- Local ethnic, language, or religious minority groups
- The LGBTIQ community
- Trade associations or unions
- Political groups and associations
- Other social organizations
- Managers of apartment complexes, workplaces, prisons

> Having a **manageable group** of Key Informants allows the BHW to train, stay in contact with, and
> provide feedback to them. Trained Key Informants help prevent the system from being overloaded
> with false or unimportant signals.

## 7. Steps in CBS at the barangay level

**Detect → Confirm → Record → Report**

### 7.1 Detecting signals

The most important detection path is a Key Informant telling the BHW about something unusual. BHWs
should also treat rumors of unusual illnesses or clusters heard from other sources as potential
signals — their own social group, church, or even conversations with strangers while shopping.

**Qualities of a good Key Informant:**
- Good knowledge of events in the community
- A broad social network, knowing lots of people in the community
- Willingness to participate and serve the community
- Ability to contact the BHW when the need arises

> It is **not necessary** for the Key Informant to have a formal education or even be able to read
> and write. They just need good awareness and understanding of what is happening around them or
> within their social groups.

**Training and orientation.** Orientation is a focused conversation where the BHW explains what
counts as a signal (Annex 1), why reporting matters, and how to reach the BHW. The BHW should give
Key Informants materials like pamphlets or posters from the local government for reference,
including the specific information needed for reporting such as a phone number to call. The BHW may
ask the nurse/midwife for support, especially if new to the job. The BHW President may invite all
Key Informants to the BHS for a group orientation.

**Rostering.** Once a Key Informant is identified and has agreed to participate, their name and
contact information should be entered into **a roster kept at the BHS**. The roster can be a simple
notebook. This helps the nurse/midwife or DSO find them if the BHW is no longer involved, or to
follow up on a reported signal.

**Follow up.** BHWs maintain ongoing relationships with Key Informants, checking in occasionally to
ensure they remember the need to report signals. During each visit, in addition to asking if they
have noticed anything unusual, the BHW should **review the signals** and **provide feedback on any
signals they may have previously reported**.

### 7.2 Confirming the information

When a signal is reported to the BHW it must be confirmed for accuracy. Details to gather:

- Ages of each of the involved individuals
- Where they lived — a complete address or description of how to find their houses
- How they are connected — same house? related? worked together? same church? all attended the same
  event, such as a wedding or other large gathering?
- When they got sick and when they died. These dates are important and should be recorded even if
  there isn't complete certainty (along with a note of why there is uncertainty)
- Was there an exposure to a sick wild or domestic animal, or something unusual in the environment,
  like a chemical spill?
- Possible reasons for their deaths

The initial report might be incomplete or incorrect — e.g. a Key Informant reports three recent
deaths, but two occurred many weeks or months ago.

### 7.3 Recording the signal

> **Every signal reported to the BHW must be recorded on a log sheet kept at the BHS, even if it
> turns out that some of the originally reported information was incorrect.**

**Screening** means checking whether the reported information meets the criteria for being a signal
(Annex 1). Signals that do not meet the criteria are still recorded on the log sheet, but marked
**'N' (not reported)** under the *Screening Result* column, with a brief description of why.

### 7.4 Reporting

Once accuracy is confirmed, the signal should be reported **immediately** to the nurse/midwife or
DSO if it fits the criteria in Annex 1. The method of reporting — SMS, WhatsApp, Viber, phone call,
or email — should have been explained by the DSO. Annex 3 gives the reporting format.

The nurse/midwife checks whether the signal matches the official list in the ESR interim guidelines.
If it does, it is reported to the MESU/CESU immediately. If not, the BHW should check back later
(e.g. after one week) to see if the situation changed.

**Parallel reporting** from BHW to nurse/midwife *and* DSO is encouraged for immediate notification,
and for instances where the nurse/midwife is too busy with other programs.

> **Case study.** BHW Gloria is making her daily rounds checking on pregnant women. Between visits
> she stops by the home office of a pastor who leads a Waray-speaking congregation. She has
> previously talked to him about the need to report signals in his congregation. After a few minutes
> talking about the weather, the pastor says: *"I don't know if this is important or not, but this
> past Sunday, several of my church members requested prayer for family members who were all sick
> and in the hospital!"*
>
> Gloria gets the names and addresses of the families and decides to visit them. It takes the rest of
> the day because they do not live close to each other, but she finds two young men and one young
> woman who attend the same youth group at the church and who all developed severe cough with fever
> in the past week. One was sick enough to be admitted to hospital. Gloria records this on the signal
> log sheet at the BHS and calls the midwife supervisor to report it.

## 8. Steps after reporting

**Verify → Assess → Respond → Feedback**

### 8.1 Verification

Verification confirms whether a signal is a real public health threat. Mainly the DSO's
responsibility, but often delegated to the nurse or midwife supervising the BHS.

> Once a signal is verified, it is called an **"event."** An ESR Reporting Form is filled out and
> entered into the ESR Information System (ESR-IS) if available. All detected signals should be
> recorded in the Signals Logsheet in the ESR-IS at the ESU level.

Verification steps:
- Checking with local health authorities to understand the illness and how widespread it is
- Asking local key informants or healthcare providers if there are more cases in the community
- Getting more details from the original source
- Checking with other sources like government departments or healthcare facilities
- Interviewing patients or family members for more information

The BHW helps by making sure the verifier knows all the important facts and introduces them to key
people. **The goal is to verify signals within 24 hours of notification.**

### 8.2 Assessment

The DSO evaluates reported events to understand their seriousness and decide what action to take —
how likely the illness is to spread, how severe it is, and whether it needs urgent reporting to
higher authorities. The assessment decides whether the event can be managed locally (a rabid dog, a
small foodborne outbreak at a wedding) or needs higher-level response (a measles or cholera
outbreak).

Important questions during assessment:
- Is the event related to a national priority disease?
- Does the suspected disease spread easily, like cholera?
- Are there deaths or severe illnesses reported?
- Could the disease affect trade or travel?

**Assessment should start within one day of verification** by the MESU/CESU team.

**Cross-notification to MAO / MVO / MENRO.** Reports must state whether exposure to animals or
environmental contamination is part of the signal. If illnesses appear linked to domestic or wild
animals, or the natural environment, this must be included in the report; the DSO cross-notifies the
Municipal Agriculture Office, Municipal Veterinary Office, or Municipal Environment and Natural
Resources Office as appropriate. The DSO also notifies MAO/MVO for a die-off of animals or a
threatening animal that appears sick, or MENRO for an environmental hazard that has not yet resulted
in human illness. Examples:

- A community member's chickens suddenly dying
- A rabid dog spotted in the area
- Many dead shorebirds or fish appearing suddenly

### 8.3 Response to the event/outbreak

Every reported event needs a response. The DSO determines the level based on the assessment. Major
responses with investigation teams from MESU/CESU might need help from the BHW who knows the
community well; minor events can be handled by the BHW.

BHWs are important health educators. Guidance by illness type:

**Respiratory infections:**
- Sick people should wear masks around others
- Consider sleeping separately if sick
- Wash hands frequently

**Intestinal infections and diarrhea:**
- Drink lots of fluids but avoid caffeine and sugary drinks
- Do not use the same eating utensils as someone who is sick without washing them first with soap
  and water
- Drink clean water and cook food properly
- Wash hands frequently

**Rabies:**
- A bite by a rabid dog is serious. Get immediate treatment to prevent rabies
- If the dog is vaccinated and stays well for 10 days after biting, the risk is over
- If the dog cannot be found, or its status is unknown, get rabies treatment as soon as possible

For serious outbreaks with deaths or hospitalizations, the DSO may conduct a full investigation. The
BHW may help by supporting affected families, tracking exposed people, and spreading disease
prevention messages.

## 9. Feedback and monitoring system performance

MESU/CESUs should give **regular feedback** to the BHS about reported signals. Similarly, BHWs
should give feedback to Key Informants or other sources of signals. Regular feedback encourages
continued reporting and improves the quality of future reports.

The MESU/CESU also monitors how well CBS is working at the BHS, calculating key indicators such as
the timeliness and accuracy of reporting shown in the BHS log sheets.

> These indicators are **not** meant to judge the BHW's work quality but reflect overall system
> performance and identify areas for improvement.

### Key indicators for monitoring at the BHS

| Category | Indicator | Target | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Timeliness** | Time from emergence of a signal/event until detection\* | **7 days** | Difference between date of signal occurrence and detection date. Calculate the median and % met target. Numerator: number of signals/events detected within 7 days of emergence. Denominator: number of signals/events detected. |
| **Timeliness** | Time from detection of a signal/event to reporting | **1 day** | Difference between detection date and reporting date. Calculate the median and % met target. Numerator: number of signals/events reported within 1 day of detection. Denominator: number of signals/events reported. |
| **Completeness** | Proportion of signals/events with complete data | **80%** | Numerator: number of signals/events recorded in the log sheet which had no missing information. Denominator: number of signals/events detected. |
| **Accuracy of reporting** | Proportion of signals screened as reportable | **50%** | Numerator: number of signals with a screening result as reportable. Denominator: number of signals detected. |
| **Management** | Proportion of BHWs with completed training on CBS in the last 12 months | **100%** | Numerator: number of registered\*\* BHWs with completed training on CBS in the last 12 months. Denominator: number of registered BHWs. |

\* *Emergence* can be counted from the earliest of: (a) date of symptom onset of the earliest case
identified in subsequent investigations; (b) date of symptom onset of the first case detected;
(c) date of identification of the first case in a facility or community; or (d) date of laboratory
diagnosis of the index case or of the first/earliest case identified.

\*\* *Registered BHW*: a BHW that is (a) entitled to benefits and incentives, (b) registered in the
Local Health Board (LHB) of the city or municipality in which they render service, and (c) part of
the National Registry of Barangay Health Workers.

## Annex 1: Signals for detection in the community

Signals are data and/or information that might represent the appearance of a potential acute health
risk, such as an outbreak. Signals may consist of reports of clusters of illness; suspect cases of
high-priority diseases; unexpected deaths (individual or aggregated); or illnesses after exposure of
human beings to animal, environment, biological, chemical, or radiological and nuclear hazards.

**The four community signal definitions** — these are the canonical list that BHWs, Key Informants
and community members screen against:

1. **Clusters of serious illness or death** — two or more people who develop a similar severe
   illness in the same 1-week period who have a social connection such as living in the same
   neighborhood, working in the same place, attending the same events, going to the same school, or
   belonging to the same social group. *A serious illness is one that is bad enough that the person
   needs hospital admission.*

2. **Clusters of severe illness in animals** — aimed at detecting large numbers of animals like
   poultry that die, or single animals that have an illness that threatens humans such as rabies or
   anthrax.

3. **Severe illness in a person who has been exposed to a sick animal** — e.g. someone who develops
   a serious lung infection after his or her chickens die, or someone who becomes sick after being
   bitten by a dog.

4. **Single individuals with certain kinds of illness (that can cause an outbreak)**, including:
   - Children with rash, fever and either of the following: cough, colds, or red eyes
   - A child under the age of 15 years who develops sudden weakness of the arms or legs
   - Anyone over the age of 2 years with severe watery diarrhea bad enough that they become
     dehydrated

### Annex 1.1: Signals for detection in the community (Filipino)

**Listahan ng mga Signal na Pangkomunidad**

1. **Cluster o grupo ng may malubhang sakit o pagkamatay:** Dalawa at mahigit pa ang nagtamo ng
   parehong malubhang karamdaman sa loob ng isang linggo at may kaugnayan sa pamamagitan ng pagiging
   magkapitbahay, magkatrabaho sa parehong lugar, pagdalo sa parehong aktibidad, pagpasok sa parehong
   paaralan o simbahan, o magkakasama sa parehong grupo o organisasyon (lupon, asosasyon, o unyon).
   Ang isang malubhang karamdaman ay matutukoy kung ang isang tao ay kinakailangan nang i-admit sa
   ospital.

2. **Cluster o grupo ng mga hayop na may malubhang sakit:** Agarang pagtukoy sa malaking bilang ng
   mga namatay na hayop (hal. manok) at isang hayop na may sakit na nagdudulot ng panganib sa mga tao
   tulad ng rabies o anthrax.

3. **Isang tao na may malubhang sakit na "na-expose" sa may sakit na hayop:** Mga halimbawa ay isang
   taong nagkaroon ng malubhang impeksyon sa baga pagkatapos mamatay ang mga alagang manok o isang
   taong nagkasakit matapos makagat ng aso.

4. **Isang kaso na may tiyak na uri ng sakit (mga sakit na maaring magdulot ng outbreak):**
   - Isang batang may rashes, lagnat at alinman sa sumusunod: ubo, sipon o pamumula ng mga mata.
   - Isang batang labinlimang taong gulang (15) pababa na biglang nakaranas ng panghihina ng braso o
     binti.
   - Isang batang dalawang taong gulang (2) pataas na nakaranas ng malubhang pagdudumi.

### Explanatory notes for the Barangay Signal Log Sheet

1. Signals are numbered consecutively as they are entered into the form.
2. Name of BHW who reported the signal.
3. Date and time of day when the signal was reported or discovered by the BHW (or other person
   working at the BHS who first heard about the signal).
4. Name and contact information of the person who reported the signal to the BHW — for example the
   Key Informant, if that is where the signal came from.
5. Describe what and how the signal happened, including signs and symptoms experienced by the cases.
6. Date of signal occurrence could be the earliest of: (1) date of symptom onset of the index case
   (first detected case); (2) date of symptom onset of the earliest case identified in subsequent
   investigations; (3) date of identification of the first case in a facility or community; or (4)
   date of laboratory result released of index case or of the earliest case.
7. Location where the signal occurred. As near as possible, record an address or other kind of
   location so that the place can be found again in case an investigation is needed.
8. List the number of any suspect involved cases; how many were admitted to hospital, and how many
   died.
9. Outcome of the screening process. Should the signal be reported to the Midwife/Nurse/DSO? Select
   **Y** for Yes, **N** for No. (*Y: Reported if the original information is confirmed to be correct
   and meets the definition of a signal. N: Not reported if the information is inaccurate or does not
   meet the signal definition.*) Provide a reason for the Y or N answer.
10. Date and time when the signal was reported to the Midwife/Nurse/DSO.
11. Describe any initial response activities or follow-up measures that were taken, such as further
    investigation, community education, etc.
12. Name of the BHW or BHS staff who is recording the information about the signal into the log
    sheet.

| Number of cases / hospitalized / deaths | Number of any suspect involved cases; how many were admitted to hospital, and how many died |

## Annex 4: Information flow between the community and the LESU

Signals flow **up** and feedback flows **back down** at every hop:

```
Key Informants
    │  signals ↓        ↑ feedback
Barangay Health Workers
    │  signals ↓        ↑ feedback
Nurses / Midwives
    │  signals ↓        ↑ feedback
MESU / CESU — Disease Surveillance Officers
```

Every level that receives a signal owes feedback to the level below it. This bidirectionality is
explicit in the guideline and is the part most often missing in practice (see the M&SS findings).

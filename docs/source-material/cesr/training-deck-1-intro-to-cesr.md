## Slide 4 — Community Event-Based Surveillance and Response (definition)

The detection and reporting of unusual health events or health risks occurring within a community, by community members including community volunteers, community health or animal health workers, the public, religious leaders, civil society members, teachers, and other similar groups.

*Speaker notes:*
Define Response (as per tech guidelines: Response: public health response refers to action and decisions carried out immediately including verification or field investigation of health events through coordination with local health authorities and other involved institutions. The nature of the response is adapted according to the nature of the public health risk.

## Slide 6 — Why do Community Event-Based Surveillance and Response (CESR)?

For the early detection of public health threats to:

- **Identify New Diseases**
  - COVID-19
  - Mpox
- **Detect Outbreaks of Preventable Diseases**
  - Measles and polio
  - Food and Water-borne diseases

*Speaker notes:*
Speaker Script:
Now you might ask—why is CESR so important?
The answer is simple: community members are the first to notice when something unusual is happening.
It could be a sudden illness in a family, a strange pattern of deaths among animals, or a cluster of symptoms affecting neighbors.
By detecting and reporting early, we can act quickly—potentially stopping outbreaks before they spread.

First, CESR plays an essential role in identifying new or emerging diseases.
These are diseases we may not have seen before, or ones appearing in a new place or form.
Examples include:
COVID-19, which began as a cluster of pneumonia cases and quickly became a global pandemic.
Mpox, formerly known as monkeypox, which re-emerged and spread to areas where it was not previously seen.
In both cases, the earliest warnings came from local observations—unusual symptoms, clusters of illness, or unfamiliar patterns.
When communities are trained and connected to a proper reporting system, we can detect these signals early, investigate them quickly, and respond effectively.

[Sub-Slide: Detecting Outbreaks of Preventable Diseases] Speaker Script:
The second area of focus is detecting outbreaks of diseases that are preventable, but still pose a risk—especially in areas with low vaccination or limited access to safe water and sanitation.
Key examples include:
Measles and Polio – Both are vaccine-preventable diseases. When cases appear, it often signals gaps in immunization coverage that need to be addressed immediately.
Food and Water-Borne Diseases – Such as cholera, typhoid, or acute diarrhea outbreaks. These are often linked to poor sanitation, unsafe water, or contaminated food—and they can spread rapidly if not contained. (clean up drive, food handling training, hygiene awareness campaign)
Again, community members are often the first to notice when something's wrong—like a group of children with rashes and fever, or multiple people in a household getting sick after a shared meal.
With CESR, these signs don't go unnoticed or unreported. They're shared early, verified, and investigated—allowing for a fast, targeted response.
"Why do we do Community Event-Based Surveillance and Response? The top reason is to detect public health threats early. CESR can help identify new diseases in the community such as COVID-19 or Mpox quickly. ## Slide 15 — What are Signals?

These are patterns that indicate that something might be going on – like seeing smoke coming out of the window of the neighbor's house. These could be:

- A sudden sickness among many people who are somehow linked (same church, neighborhood, workplace, school)
- A single case of a high-threat disease (eg. measles, cholera, or a rabid dog)

*Speaker notes:*
"Signals are patterns that indicate something unusual might be happening. They can include a sudden increase of sick individuals in a group or community who are somehow connected—like being from the same church, neighborhood, workplace, or school.

A signal can also be a single case of a high-threat disease that is highly contagious and causes severe illness, such as measles, cholera, or even a rabid dog. Just one case of these can quickly lead to an outbreak or result in high mortality, which is why it needs to be contained and controlled immediately."

## Slide 16 — What is an Outbreak?

Multiple people in the community getting sick around the same time.

Indicate that a disease-causing organism is spreading around through:

- Person-to-person contact
- Animal-to-person contact
- From the environment or other media

*Speaker notes:*
"Outbreaks, on the other hand, refer to many people getting sick around the same time. This can be caused by various factors and spreading routes, including person-to-person contact, animal-to-person contact, and from the environment and other media."

*Instructor Tips:*
Give examples of transmission routes:
- Person-to-person contact: respiratory droplets from coughing or sneezing (e.g., influenza, COVID-19).
- Animal-to-person contact: bites or direct contact with animals (e.g., rabies, H5N1 avian influenza).
- Environment: contaminated water or food (e.g., cholera, salmonella, leptospirosis).
- Insect bites: transmission by vectors such as mosquitoes (e.g., malaria, dengue).

## Slide 17 — SIGNALS

- Signals indicate possible community health threats – such as disease outbreaks.
- Like smoke from a window, signals suggest a potential issue needing investigation.
- It's important to report real and impactful signals, regardless of the cause.

*Speaker notes:*

## Slide 22 — Signal List for the Community: 2. CLUSTERS OF SEVERE ILLNESS IN ANIMALS

This aims to detect significant occurrences such as:

- Large-scale poultry deaths
- Animals with diseases that pose human threats:
  - Rabies
  - Anthrax

Signal Example: a number of a local resident's chickens have died unexpectedly

*Speaker notes:*
"The second signal to watch for is Cluster of Severe Illness in Animals. This includes important events such as large numbers of poultry dying unexpectedly, and animals with diseases that pose human threats, such as rabies and anthrax.
For example, if a resident notices several of their chickens have suddenly died, this could be a signal. It is important to report it right away so it can be checked and addressed quickly."

*Instructor Tips:*
- Explain why severe illness in animals is an important signal. Mention that some animal diseases can spread to humans (zoonotic diseases) or indicate environmental problems that might affect both animals and humans.
- Provide examples from their community, if available, to make the concept more relatable. For instance, mention specific instances where animal illnesses were linked to human disease outbreaks in the past.
- Explain that anthrax is a bacterial disease that can affect animals like cows, goats, and sheep, and sometimes humans. It is usually found in soil and can infect grazing animals, leading to sudden death, often without prior signs of illness. Humans can get anthrax by handling sick animals, contaminated meat, wool, or hides, or by inhaling bacterial spores. While rare, it can be serious if not addressed quickly. Early reporting and proper handling of animals and meat prevent the disease from spreading.

## Slide 25 — Signal List for the Community: 3. SEVERE ILLNESS IN AN INDIVIDUAL WHO HAS BEEN EXPOSED TO A SICK ANIMAL

Signal Example:
Someone who develops a serious lung infection after his or her chickens die or someone who becomes sick after being bitten by a dog

*Speaker notes:*
"The third signal to watch for is Severe Illness in an Individual who has been exposed to a Sick Animal. This means a person became seriously ill after contact with an animal that is not unwell. For example, if someone develops a serious lung infection after their chickens die suddenly, or if someone gets sick after being bitten by a dog, this should raise concern. These situations could indicate diseases like bird flu, or rabies, which can spread from animals to people.
If you notice this signal, it's important to report it immediately so health workers can investigate and take action to protect the community. Remember, the connection between the sick person and the animal is the key part of identifying this signal."

*Instructor Tips:*
Examples of contact or exposure to animals:
- Touching dead animals with the disease: For instance, handling a chicken carcass that died suddenly without clear cause could expose a person to diseases like avian flu.
- Eating a dead animal: Consuming meat from an animal that died of illness can lead to serious infections, such as anthrax or foodborne illnesses.
- Animal bites: Illness after being bitten by a dog, especially one behaving unusually, might indicate rabies.
- Handling sick animals: A person caring for or cleaning up after an ill animal, like livestock, could be exposed to harmful bacteria or viruses.
- Exposure to contaminated environments: Cleaning areas with animal droppings or carcasses may expose individuals to diseases like Salmonellosis, Brucellosis, and Hantavirus.
- Highlight the importance of asking about recent contact with animals when investigating severe illness.

## Slide 36 — What to do with signals?

Finding a signal triggers actions such as investigation and verification.

Asking more questions:

- How many people affected?
- If there were multiple people, how were they connected? Live together? Work together? Go to school together?
- How sick are the people involved? Any deaths or hospital admissions?
- Were there any animals involved?
- Has anyone affected traveled outside of the area recently?

*Speaker notes:*
(Ask) "After knowing what signals are, what do we do with them?"
(Pass the mic to one participant and collect responses.)

\<Click\>
"We need to do a preliminary investigation and confirmation. We can ask the following:"
\<Click\>
"We can ask how many are affected, how they are related, how sick are the people involved, any deaths or hospital admissions? Are there animals affected as well, and if anyone affected traveled outside of the community?

*Instructor Tips:*
- After reading the slide title, ask the audience what they think the steps are after detecting a signal. Collect their answers and discuss if they are relevant to the surveillance and response.
- Remind participants to investigate by asking key questions including: number of cases; common activities, location, or interaction among the cases; outcomes of the cases, i.e. hospitalization and death.
- Discuss if a signal is not verified/confirmed, it can lead to several serious consequences:
  - Missed early warnings: potential health threats might go unnoticed, leading to delayed responses and more severe outbreaks.
  - False alarms: Unverified signals can cause unnecessary panic and resource allocation to non-issues.
  - Resource wastage: time, money, and effort might be spent on investigating false signals instead of real threats.
  - Loss of trust: frequent false alarms can erode public trust in health systems and authorities.
  - Inaccurate data: unverified signals can lead to incorrect data, affecting decision-making and resource allocation.

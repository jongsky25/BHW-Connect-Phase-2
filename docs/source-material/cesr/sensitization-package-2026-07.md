## Introduction to Community Based Disease Surveillance (1)

- Public health threats can emerge anywhere — in homes, schools, farms, markets, or in every
  community setting.
- Many outbreaks begin with unusual events that are noticed by community members before they
  appear in health facility data.
- Such as clusters of fever and rashes, clusters of diarrhea after a community gathering, cluster
  of cough and colds in daycare center or school, or unusual sudden deaths of water birds in a
  river.
- Facility-based surveillance alone cannot detect every public health threat, especially in
  geographically isolated or underserved communities.

## Introduction to Community Based Disease Surveillance (2)

- Delays in detection lead to more cases and larger outbreaks.
- Every delay allows the disease to spread further, putting more lives at risk.

*Speaker notes:* This figure illustrates why early detection is so important. By the time an
outbreak is recognized through health facility data, transmission may already be well underway.
Community members often notice unusual events first, long before they are reflected in routine
surveillance.

If communities are often the first to observe unusual events, how can we systematically capture
these signals and translate them into timely public health action? This is where Community
Event-Based Surveillance and Response (CESR) plays a critical role.

If outbreaks begin in communities before they reach our surveillance systems, the next question
is: What are the unusual events that communities can detect and report? This is where CESR
introduces community event signals.

## What is Community Event-Based Surveillance and Response (CESR)?

CESR is a community-based surveillance system that empowers community members to look and report
signals or unusual health events, enabling health authorities to verify signals and initiate
timely public health action.

*Speaker notes:* Community Event-Based Surveillance and Response, or CESR, is a community-based,
event-focused early warning system that extends disease surveillance beyond health facilities and
into the community.

It empowers trained community members — such as Barangay Health Workers, teachers, community
leaders, farmers, and other trusted individuals — to recognize and report unusual health events or
signals that may indicate a potential public health threat.

As shown in this process, CESR begins when community members observe something unusual. They
report the signal through a simple reporting pathway, after which health authorities verify the
information, assess the public health risk, and initiate an appropriate response if needed.

## Who are key informants in the community?

Key informants are community members from different sectors who notice or hear about unusual
health events and report them for early action to protect our community.

### How CESR Complements Routine Surveillance

Routine surveillance and CESR are complementary systems. Routine surveillance starts at health
facilities and monitors diagnosed cases, while CESR starts in the community by identifying unusual
events or signals before they appear in routine data.

These early reports are verified, integrated into the surveillance system, and used to guide risk
assessment and timely public health action. Together, they strengthen early detection, improve
decision-making, and support faster outbreak response.

## What is a signal?

A signal is any information about an event that may indicate a potential risk to health.

- Signals come from community observations and reports, even before the event is confirmed.
- Early signals allow health workers to verify, investigate and act before an outbreak happens.

### The four signal types

| # | Signal type | Example |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Cluster of Serious Illness or Death | Clustering of diarrhea and vomiting in a gathering |
| 2 | Cluster of Severe Illness in Animals or Death | Unusual poultry deaths |
| 3 | Individual Illness who has been exposed to animals | High fever and difficulty breathing after poultry deaths |
| 4 | Individuals with certain kinds of illness (outbreak potential) | Child with rash, fever, and either cough, or red eyes |

*Speaker notes:* At the heart of CESR is the concept of a signal. A signal is any information about
an unusual event that may indicate a potential risk to public health. At this stage, it is not yet
a confirmed case or an outbreak — it is simply something unusual that deserves attention.

Communities are often the first to observe these signals, allowing health authorities to verify
them and respond before an outbreak grows. In CESR, we monitor four main signal types: clusters of
serious illness or death in people, unusual illness or deaths in animals, severe illness after
exposure to sick animals, and specific illnesses with outbreak potential.

## CESR Process

| Role | Action |
| :-- | :-- |
| Key Informant | Notices or hears about an unusual health event that affects humans or animals or the environment |
| Barangay Health Worker (BHW) | Listens, verifies information, and record the report. Inform the BHS Supervisor. |
| Barangay Health Station (BHS) Supervisor | Reviews the report. Verifies, assess the risks, and reports to CESU/MESU. |
| CESU/MESU | Receives the report, validates, investigates, and coordinate the response. |

*Speaker notes:* Now that we've identified the four types of signals, let's look at what happens
after a signal is reported — from reporting and verification to investigation and public health
response.

## CESR Case Studies

Timeline legend: Signal Occurrence to Report → Report to Verification → Verification to Early
Response Initiation.

### Case study 1 — Clustering of Influenza-like-Illness in a Public Secondary School, Sta. Cruz, Marinduque

- Initial cluster of cough and colds among Grade 7 students
- Signal occurrence to report: **8 days**
- Report to verification: **<1 day**
- School Principal reported to BHW
- MESU conducted field investigation:
  - Total: **128 cases**, females (**59%**)
  - Age range: **11–54 years old** (median: **15**)
  - Most affected: Grade 7 (**43, 34%**)
- Response: suspension of classes; school disinfection; policies on minimum public health standards
  (mask and cough etiquette)
- Verification to early response initiation: **2 days** / **1 day**

### Case study 2 — Clustering of Measles at Brgy Tatalon, Quezon City

- 4-year-old, male, with fever, maculopapular rashes and cough
- Mother of the case reported to BHW
- DSO conducted field investigation:
  - **16 Measles cases** were identified, **13 (81%)** were Laboratory Confirmed
  - Most are males (**63%**), age range from **5 months–39 years old** (median: **6**)
- Response: contact tracing; monitoring of close contacts; Outbreak Response Immunization
- Timeline intervals recorded: **3 days** / **4 days**

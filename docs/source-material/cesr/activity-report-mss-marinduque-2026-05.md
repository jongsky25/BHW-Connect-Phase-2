## Activity highlights

### Surveillance operations and data management

| Key findings | Recommendations |
|---|---|
| **Coverage of signal recording ranges from 4% (Sta. Cruz, 2/55) to 64% (Torrijos, 16/25).** | Institutionalize active monitoring of barangays with no reported signals to improve surveillance sensitivity. |
| **BHWs demonstrated gaps in recalling CESR signal definitions**, potentially affecting signal detection quality. | Conduct refresher training on CESR signal definitions, detection, recording, and reporting procedures. |
| While a standardized CESR logbook is available, **different approaches to signal recording were observed across different BHS.** | Standardize logbook documentation and reporting practices across implementation sites. |
| Signals were generally reported within one day after verification by the midwife, **primarily through submission of logbook photos via Messenger.** | **Establish and maintain a centralized database for signal management, analysis, and monitoring.** |
| Except for Torrijos, MESUs do not routinely conduct active monitoring of BHS with no reported signals. Signal data is consolidated at the MESU level; however, **reporting remains largely image-based and lacks a functional database.** Although PESU/PDOHO established a Google Sheet for signal encoding, **utilization remains limited, with only a few entries recorded.** | Institutionalize weekly reporting of signals from BHS to MESU. |
| **MESU are unable to distinguish whether the absence of reports reflects the absence of signals or a failure to report.** | Reporting template per BHS to MESU **with zero reporting (every Friday)**. Tag BHS with **3 consecutive weeks of no reports as "silent" BHS.** MESU shall conduct visits to BHS tagged as "silent" or with 3 consecutive zero reports. |
| Feedback mechanisms are primarily provided for verified health events, **with limited routine feedback to reporting units.** | Strengthen routine feedback mechanisms between MESU and BHSs, **including signals determined to be non-reportable.** |
| Animal and environmental sectors are not yet fully integrated as key informants despite the occurrence of animal health events. | **Creation of a CESR Signal Dashboard** that will serve as a surveillance report distributed to stakeholders. Expand and formalize participation of animal and agricultural sector stakeholders as key informants. |

### Workforce capacity and support

| Key findings | Recommendations |
|---|---|
| Signal detection and reporting integration into routine BHS workflows remains inconsistent. | Integrate CESR activities into standard BHS workflows and routine supervisory processes. MESU to continue refresher sessions and monitoring using the BHS checklist to encourage BHWs in reporting signals. |
| Some midwives and key informants have not yet received CESR orientation or training. | Conduct targeted training and refresher activities for midwives, BHWs, and key informants. |
| MESU personnel continue to manage multiple programs, limiting dedicated attention to surveillance activities. | Advocate for additional human resources or dedicated surveillance support staff at the municipal level. |

### Logistics and operational resources

| Key findings | Recommendations |
|---|---|
| Additional IEC materials, **particularly Tagalog versions**, were requested across sites. | EpiC GHS to support the reproduction and distribution of additional CESR IEC materials. |
| Lack of dedicated transportation, limited office supplies, and **connectivity constraints** continue to affect surveillance operations. | Lobby for LGU budget allocation to improve connectivity and communication support for CESR implementation in geographically isolated areas. |
| **Additional manpower is needed for signal encoding and data management.** | LGU-generated data on CESR should be developed and established to be used as an advocacy tool for manpower needs — crafted using insights gathered during the advocacy visit to the Gasan Mayor. |

## Health events review

| Health event | Description of cases and health event | Response actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Paragonimiasis, Sta. Cruz, Marinduque** | First case: 17-year-old female, onset August 2025, symptoms of cough that worsens at night, hemoptysis, body malaise. **The signal was reported by a BHW of the Rural Health Unit.** Case finding revealed 170 (65%) tested positive for *Paragonimus* ova out of 259 tested by sputum smear microscopy — mostly male (94, 55%), female (76, 45%). Age range 8–78 years (median 63). No deaths. Barangays with the most cases: Napo 14 (8%), Alobo 11 (6%), Taytay 9 (5%). | Field investigation, contact tracing, and records review of TB cases. Paragonimiasis prevention IEC through social media posting, barangay assemblies and radio announcement highlighting symptoms and proper food preparation and cooking practices. |
| **Clustering of influenza-like illness in a public secondary high school, Sta. Cruz** | School principal reported to the BHW of Landy BHS an increasing number of students and teachers with fever, cough and colds. The signal was reported by the BHW to MESU Sta. Cruz. Onset 12 October 2025, initially a group of Grade 7 students. Investigation identified 128 cases — female (76, 59%), male (52, 41%). Age 11–54 (median 15). No hospitalizations. 124 (97%) students, 4 (3%) teaching personnel. Grade 7 (43, 34%) highest, then Grade 11 (27, 21%) and Grade 12 (25, 20%). | Contact tracing and active case finding. Suspension of classes and school disinfection. Release of policies and memorandum on face mask use, respiratory etiquette and minimum public health standards on respiratory illness prevention. Public health risk communication and community engagement. |
| **Confirmed rabies in dog, Barangay Malibago, Torrijos** | A rabid stray dog bit four individuals, including children. **The signal was reported by the Barangay Kagawad on Health to the BHW.** Signal was immediately recorded and reported to the midwife and MESU. Cases were brought to the Animal Bite Treatment Clinic of RHU Main Torrijos. Two victims were Category III but **there was no supply of Equine Rabies Immunoglobulin (ERIG)**; cases were referred to Boac ABTC where they were vaccinated with anti-rabies vaccine. | Immediate case investigation. Case finding. Ring vaccination. Profiling of cats and dogs in the barangay. Community engagement on responsible pet ownership and rabies prevention. Presentation of the health event to the Local Health Board and Peace and Order Council to lobby for additional funding for rabies vaccines and other logistical needs. |

## Conclusion

- **CESR brings One Health and surveillance principles close to the frontlines.** The RESU, PESU,
  MESU and barangays visited have all expressed their appreciation of CESR. When early implementation
  challenges are addressed, CESR will be critical in improving not just the early detection of health
  events, but the community's awareness and understanding of outbreak prevention, detection and
  response.

- **Institutionalizing CESR in both local surveillance processes and health staff workflows is
  essential to quality implementation.** As observed in Torrijos and Sta. Cruz, including CESR in
  routine surveillance workflows (MESUs' routine monitoring, BHW routine activities) as well as
  having an organized system for it (orientation workshop for KIs, data validation) facilitates early
  health event detection and response. Signal detection, recording, verification, and reporting
  should become standard components of daily community health activities. Integrating CESR
  responsibilities into regular BHW household visits, community outreach activities, barangay
  assemblies, and routine supervisory processes will help ensure sustained surveillance performance.

- **Continuous and structured monitoring and supportive supervision activities are critical.**
  Observing the gaps that persist in implementation post-training, it is critical for M&SS activities
  to continue beyond a single visit. Structured and routine M&SS must be established across
  government levels — from EB to RESU, RESU to PESU, PESU to MESU and MESU to Barangay. Priority
  activities should focus on strengthening signal surveillance systems through standardized recording
  and reporting practices, routine analysis of CESR data, refresher capacity-building for midwives,
  active monitoring of silent barangays, and expansion of key informant networks. **Establishing
  functional signal surveillance databases and regular feedback mechanisms between MESUs and
  barangays will further improve surveillance sensitivity, timeliness, and data quality. In addition,
  the development of municipal-level signal surveillance dashboards and analyses will provide
  valuable evidence for decision-making and preparedness.**

- **Conducting health event reviews provides significant insight into response quality.** The
  reviews provided valuable insight not just on 7-1-7 timelines, but on what went well and what could
  be improved, and into systemic issues and gaps. The review of paragonimiasis, influenza-like
  illness, and rabies highlighted how signals detected and reported by barangay-level informants
  contributed to timely verification, investigation, and response. These findings reinforce the value
  of CESR as an essential component of local surveillance systems and a key mechanism for
  strengthening early warning and rapid response capacities.

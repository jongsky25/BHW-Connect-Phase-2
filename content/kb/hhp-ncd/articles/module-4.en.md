# Safe capillary blood glucose testing in the household

Capillary blood glucose testing measures the sugar in a drop of blood from a fingerstick, read by a handheld meter. It is a screening test: diabetes is diagnosed at the facility with laboratory tests. Because it involves blood, the safety rules are not optional extras — they are the procedure.

## Before you start

Anyone performing capillary sampling must be trained in infection prevention and assessed as competent first; the authority to do it in your barangay comes from your LGU and supervising nurse or midwife. Check that you have gloves, single-use lancets, in-date strips in a closed vial, a working meter, alcohol swabs, dry gauze and a puncture-proof sharps container placed within arm's reach before the first client. If any of these is missing, stop and report the stockout — do not improvise.

Ask and record whether the test is fasting or random, and the time of the last meal. The two are read against different values.

## The procedure

1. Perform hand hygiene, then put on well-fitting non-sterile gloves. One pair per client, never reused.
2. Choose the side of the fingertip on the middle or ring finger. Avoid the thumb and index finger, the very tip, and any finger that is cold, swollen, infected, scarred or sore from previous tests.
3. If the hand is cold, warm it and let the arm hang down for a moment. Never force blood out by hard squeezing.
4. Clean the site with alcohol and let it dry completely before pricking.
5. Use a sterile single-use lancet — preferably a safety lancet that retracts automatically, which prevents both reuse and needlestick injury. A lancet is never used twice, on anyone.
6. Wipe away the first drop with dry gauze and apply the next drop to the strip.
7. Discard the whole lancet as one unit straight into the sharps container.
8. Remove gloves and perform hand hygiene again.

## Keeping the result trustworthy

Store strips in their original vial with the cap closed, away from heat, sun and damp, and check the expiry before each session. Run the control solution check as the manufacturer directs — routinely, after a new vial, after a battery change, and whenever a result does not fit the person in front of you. If the meter shows an error or an unbelievable number, do not write it down and do not tell the client a value you do not trust: repeat once with a fresh strip, and if it still fails, refer for testing at the facility and report the device.

## If something goes wrong

If you prick yourself, let the wound bleed briefly, wash with soap and running water, do not scrub or apply bleach, cover it, and report to your supervisor the same day so exposure follow-up can start. Treat all blood as infectious: cover spills, clean with the disinfectant provided while gloved, and dispose of used materials as infectious waste.

## Acting on the result

Refer immediately, with a companion, for signs of very high blood sugar — heavy thirst with frequent urination, vomiting, deep or laboured breathing, abdominal pain, drowsiness, a fruity smell on the breath — or of low blood sugar: shakiness, sweating, hunger, palpitations, confusion. If a person with low sugar is awake and can swallow, give sugar or a sweet drink at once, then food, then refer. If unconscious, give nothing by mouth and arrange emergency transport.

The numeric referral cut-offs for community screening come from the current PhilPEN protocol as adopted by your LGU.

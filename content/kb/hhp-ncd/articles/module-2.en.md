# Structured risk assessment with the NCD Flipchart

The NCD Flipchart is the DOH–WHO job aid covering hypertension, diabetes, cancer and chronic lung disease. In community screening it does two jobs at the same time: it gives structure to the questions you ask, and its client-facing pages let you teach the household while you assess them.

## Why structure matters more than speed

The value of a risk assessment lies in it being the *same* assessment everywhere. When every BHW asks the same items in the same order, the results can be compared between households, between barangays and over time — which is what lets the region see whether the programme is working. Asking in the printed order also has two practical benefits: easy, non-threatening questions come first and sensitive ones later, once the client is comfortable; and you do not discover a skipped item after the visit has ended.

## What the assessment covers

- Identity, age and sex
- Personal and family history of hypertension, diabetes and heart disease
- Tobacco and alcohol use
- Diet and physical activity
- Physical measurements — blood pressure, weight and height, waist circumference
- Symptom screening
- The risk finding and the recommended action

Use the current form issued by your LGU. Do not reconstruct the questions from memory, and do not add or drop items on your own judgement.

## Asking well

Sensitive items — smoking, alcohol, weight — are answered honestly only when they are asked as ordinary health questions, without a lecture attached. Ask about frequency and amount rather than identity: how many days a week, how much on a typical day, rather than "are you a drinker". Save the advice for the counselling step, when the person is ready to hear it.

If the client does not know an answer, write "not known". If they decline, record it as declined and move on. Screening is voluntary, and pressing a person risks the measurements and the referral — which matter far more than any single line on the form.

## Findings that change the urgency

Some findings turn a routine referral into an immediate one. Arrange transport now, with a companion, for:

- chest pain or tightness
- difficulty breathing at rest
- one-sided weakness or numbness, facial droop, slurred speech
- sudden loss of vision, seizure, confusion, fainting
- a very high reading together with symptoms

In these situations the form can be completed afterwards.

## Keep the result and the referral consistent

The assessment tool and your referral recommendation must agree, and the protocol outranks impression. If the tool says refer, refer — even if the person looks well. If it does not, but you saw a warning sign, refer anyway and write down the sign you saw. Never downgrade a referral because the client asked you to.

## Record as you go

Numbers written from memory after several households are the single most common source of bad data in community screening. Write the readings on the spot, and finish the rest of the form before you leave the yard.

# Documentation, reporting and cascading the skill

Your register is where community screening turns into programme data. Coverage figures, blood pressure control rates and the case for continued funding are all built from barangay-level records, and a facility deciding on a person's treatment reads what you wrote. The chain starts at your handwriting.

## The screening register

Record, for every person: date, identifying details, age and sex, the risk findings, every measurement with its unit, whether a referral was made and how urgent, the agreed follow-up date, and your name. Use the register issued for the programme rather than a personal notebook, so the entries line up with what the facility reports upward.

Write as you go. Numbers reconstructed from memory after several households are the most common source of bad data in this work.

## Corrections and gaps

To correct a mistake, draw a single line through the wrong entry so it stays readable, write the correct value beside it, and add your initials and the date. Never erase, never use correction fluid, never write over a number — an unreadable correction looks like an alteration and puts the whole record in doubt.

If you find later that a form is incomplete, do not fill the gap from memory. Mark the item as not obtained, and if it matters — a measurement, a referral instruction — go back or contact the client and note the date you completed it. An honest blank is usable; an invented value is not.

## Closing the referral loop

A referral without follow-up is a piece of paper. Keep a list of everyone you referred with the date, ask the midwife or nurse for the return slip or feedback, and visit or contact the client on the agreed date. Report anyone still outstanding after several weeks, so the facility can plan an outreach day or a home visit. People lost between the screening and the first consultation are where this programme leaks the most.

## Keeping records safe

Screening results are personal health information. Keep forms closed and out of reach of the household and visitors — not loose in a bag, not on a table at home, not photographed into a chat. Turn completed forms over to the health centre on schedule, and never hold personal health information longer than your role requires. If you stop being a BHW or transfer barangay, hand over all registers, pending referrals and follow-up lists, with a note on who is still awaiting follow-up.

## Cascading the skill

The cascade only works if the first link is accurate. Before you certify training in your barangay you must, at minimum, have passed the knowledge and practical competency assessment yourself, be using the current standardised materials, run the session with demonstration and return demonstration, assess each participant against the checklist, and document who was trained and on what.

Return demonstration is the method: you show the skill step by step, then the learner performs it back while you observe against a checklist. Watching a video does not prove anyone can take a blood pressure correctly. Observe silently, mark each step as it happens rather than from memory, and do not coach mid-assessment — give feedback afterwards, with a date to repeat if steps were missed.

Do not teach anything outside BHW scope — interpreting laboratory tests, deciding on medicines, giving a diagnosis — and do not teach shortcuts you invented, however practical they seem. Where the package is silent, say so and ask the RHU rather than filling the gap yourself.

Document every session: date and place, facilitator, attendance with signatures, modules covered, materials version, checklist results per participant, and the follow-up agreed for anyone who did not meet the standard. An undocumented session cannot be counted or verified.

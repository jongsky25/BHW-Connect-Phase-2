## [scenario/core] Three visits, the same barrier {m7.define}

BHW Nestor, 41, revisits three households that missed an agreed health-center visit. Instead of labeling them “uncooperative,” he records dates, missed visits and the residents’ stated reasons. These three households are a training example, not data from your barangay. The problem is inability to reach a service on the agreed day; its causes still need investigation before choosing a solution.

:::takeaway
Define the problem using events and evidence rather than judgments about people.
:::

## [concept/core] Every why needs evidence {m7.whys}

Ask why the visit was missed, then why the reported reason arose. In this fictional example, childcare was unavailable; the schedule overlapped work; only one time was communicated; household availability had not been checked. These are possible explanations to confirm with residents, BHWs and health-center staff. Reaching exactly five whys is not the goal. Reach an actionable cause supported by evidence; follow separate branches when there is more than one cause.

:::visual 1
:::takeaway
Five Whys investigates evidence-based causes with others instead of repeating blame.
:::
:::check
? The answer is “no transport money.” What next?
- Record the family as lazy.
+ Ask and check which costs and access barriers are involved.
- Stop investigating because five questions have been asked.
> Investigate the explanation with the household; the number of questions does not establish a root cause.
:::

## [concept/core] Make priorities explicit {m7.prioritize}

For planning, score each problem from 1 to 5 on four criteria: how many are affected, severity of the effects, feasibility with available resources, and urgency. A higher feasibility score means more achievable. Add the four scores and discuss their evidence with the group. A high total suggests a starting priority, not permission to ignore serious danger. Refer someone needing urgent care immediately rather than waiting for a planning score.

:::visual 2
:::takeaway
Priorities require explicit criteria and local judgment; they do not replace immediate referral.
:::

## [contrast/core] Match the solution to the cause {m7.solutions}

If scheduling and childcare are the barriers, another poster may be insufficient. Compare two proposals: a poster alone, or discussion with the health center and households about a more accessible schedule. Which addresses the verified cause? Who can implement it? What time, people and costs are involved? Do not promise a new service or funding until the responsible authority agrees.

:::takeaway
Compare solutions against the cause and resources, not convenience alone.
:::
:::check
? Scheduling is the verified main barrier. Which proposal addresses it better?
+ Discuss a possible alternative time with the health center and households.
- Repeat the same reminder more often.
- Accept all missed visits without follow-up.
> It addresses the cause while recognizing that the health center must agree to the change.
:::

## [practice/core] Turn a proposal into agreed work {m7.recommend, m7.mobilize}

Nestor proposes a simple plan: consult affected households this week, send aggregated preferred times to the midwife, and discuss one trial schedule with the barangay health planning team. Name an owner, resources, deadline and measure, such as attendance among those invited. On the review date, ask whether the barrier decreased, not merely whether a meeting happened. Confirm each partner’s contribution first.

:::takeaway
A recommendation becomes a plan when it names an owner, resources, date and outcome measure.
:::

## [concept/standard] The manual’s diarrhoea example {m7.diarrhoea-example}

The Reference Manual uses a child’s diarrhoea death to investigate connected barriers to care and safe water. In the deck’s sample ranking, gaps in BHW training and teaching safe drinking-water preparation rank highly. This does not make training the cause of every death. Check explanations against evidence; poverty, transport and service gaps may also contribute. The example examines a system, not treatment instructions.

:::takeaway
A worked example teaches a method; real priorities come from local evidence.
:::

## [scenario/standard] Rosario is a child {m7.determinants}

In the Zuellig Family Foundation case study, Rosario is a child aged one year and four months. Her death is linked to measles with diarrhoea, dehydration and malnutrition amid an unsafe environment, low income, misinformation and barriers to services. Draw separate branches for environment, livelihood and health services. Do not blame the mother alone or turn medicines mentioned in the historical story into current medical advice.

:::takeaway
Illness has a context: water, income, information and access to services interact.
:::

## [practice/standard] Try scoring priorities

Fictional practice: a BHW training gap scores 4 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 19; a safe-water teaching gap scores 4 + 3 + 5 + 5 = 17; a household water-preparation knowledge gap scores 3 + 3 + 5 + 5 = 16. The order is magnitude, severity, feasibility, urgency. These are the deck’s example scores, not measurements of your barangay. Name the evidence needed before using each score in a real plan.

:::takeaway
Explain the scores before trusting the total.
:::
:::check
? Two problems both score 17. What should the group do?
- Choose whichever is listed first.
- Raise the leader’s preferred score without explanation.
+ Review evidence, urgency and resources with the group.
> A tie calls for discussion rather than an arbitrary ranking.
:::

## [practice/standard] Who should take part?

A BHW cannot alone fix transport, water and service schedules. On the problem map, mark actions within the BHW’s role, issues for the health team, and needs requiring barangay or other agencies. Include affected residents, particularly those who seldom attend meetings. Report patterns without unnecessary personal details and return to residents with the agreement.

:::takeaway
Solutions need owners and partners; not every task belongs to the BHW.
:::

## [practice/deep] When the first plan does not work

The schedule moved earlier but two households still missed the visit. Do not label the families as failures. Recheck barriers: did work, costs or notice change? Compare invitations and attendance before and after the trial. Identify which assumption was wrong and what small change to try. One small trial does not prove the whole problem is solved.

:::takeaway
Review uses results to revise assumptions and the plan.
:::

## [practice/deep] Build your own problem tree

Using the fictional missed-visit case, place a clear problem at the center. Put verified causes below it and distinguish those still needing investigation. Place effects above. Choose one branch, compare two solutions and present a one-minute recommendation. If using a local example, remove names and identifying details before sharing.

:::takeaway
A recommendation should trace back to a cause and evidence.
:::

## [missed-visits] Three households: what happened?

BHW Carole listens to three households reporting that they missed an agreed health-center visit. This is a fictional classroom example, not data from your barangay. The first resident says, “I could not attend on Monday.” The second says, “I had work.” The third says, “The day was unclear to me.” These are their reports, not proof of one shared barrier. No appointment card or attendance record has been shown. Carole records who spoke, what was reported and which date still needs clarification. The initial problem is nonattendance at the agreed visit. She does not label the families “uncooperative” or choose a cause and solution yet.

## [worked-example] Separate the problem from a guess

“Three households missed visits” describes the event in the example; add that this comes from their reports. “They did not want to go” guesses at motivation. “We need a poster” proposes a solution. A report does not establish that people do not want to get well. Carole asks, “What actually happened? Who reported it? What do we still not know?” A poster might help, but first investigate whether it relates to the reported barrier. Sort the statements into separate categories: event, guess and solution. Before choosing a solution, listen to each household and identify a record that could clarify the situation.

## [source-gap] Whose report, which gap?

Use three columns: event and scope, source, and information gap. In the first: three households in the example reported missing a visit. In the second: each resident spoke directly to Carole; she did not personally observe attendance. In the third: exact dates, agreed visit days and each household’s barrier. “I had work” is the second resident’s report, not everyone’s cause. No record has been shown, so do not claim the count or cancellation has been verified. In actual work, use only relevant information and seek clarification from a person authorized to access the record under local procedures. This lesson creates no new access rule.

## [resident-perspective] Let the resident correct the account

Carole asks the first resident, “What happened on the visit day? What should I correct in my understanding?” The resident replies, “I meant Tuesday, not Monday. I do not know whether the center canceled it.” Carole says, “According to you, you missed Tuesday; cancellation is uncertain. Is that an accurate summary?” The resident replies, “Yes, that is what I said.” Carole corrects the reported date and retains the source of the correction. This does not confirm attendance or cancellation. Listen to the other residents separately; retain differences between accounts. Silence, a nod or an expression does not prove agreement or motivation.

## [practice] Try, give feedback, then retry

Write: “Three households in the example reported to Carole that they missed agreed visits. The first resident corrected the reported day to Tuesday; the attendance record, exact dates and each household’s barrier remain unchecked.” Compare this with “The families lack discipline.” Which parts are event, source and gap? In triads, rotate as BHW, resident and observer: write, ask without blame, receive a correction, give feedback and retry. Everyone attempts the BHW role. Alone, use the same fictional cards and checklist; write first, compare with the answer, then revise. In your next report, seek the affected residents’ perspectives too. An unwitnessed attempt is not observed, not automatically passed.

## [check] Choose the next step

First look at the illustration of listening and correction. Choose the statement that appropriately starts an investigation and explain why before viewing the feedback. Compare all answers with your own attempt. The ending should contain an event and scope, a source and an honest gap; it should not invent a cause or promise a solution.

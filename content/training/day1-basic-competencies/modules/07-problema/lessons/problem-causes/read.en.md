## [whys] Every why needs evidence

Carole, a BHW in a fictional classroom exercise, asks about missed visits by three households. Each household’s reason is not yet known. She listens to residents and health-center staff before recommending changes. This count is not barangay data.

The resident on card A says: “I had no one to look after my child at that time.” Card B says: “I did not have enough fare that day.” Card C has no reply yet. Draw two possible branches: childcare/timing and travel costs. Do not assume the same cause for every household. A person’s statement is a report, not proof of every arrow.

Ask: “What happened before that? Who could explain it, or what authorized record could help?” Work overlap, one communicated time and household availability not checked are possible explanations still to investigate. Label each arrow with its source and reported, verified or to check. Exactly five questions are not proof. Stop when there is enough evidence for a proportionate next check; keep alternative branches open.

## [manual-example] The manual’s diarrhoea example

The DOH Reference Manual follows whys through an example of a child who died with diarrhoea. Its worked example connects water, information and gaps in BHW training. The deck’s example table places BHW training and education on preparing drinking water first. These are source examples, not verified causes of every death.

Show interacting water, income, transport, information and access to services. For each arrow, ask which source passage supports it and what still needs local investigation. A child’s illness does not prove that a parent lacks knowledge. Do not blame the family or assume a lecture is sufficient before investigating barriers.

This historical example is separate from Carole’s fictional missed visits. It teaches systems analysis, not diagnosis, medicines or treatment. The lesson does not claim that training causes every death or that a local solution has been approved.

## [rosario] Rosario is a child

## [verify-branch] Verify and correct a branch

Carole’s first map has an arrow: “only one time offered → missed visit.” It is still to check. She asks the resident on card A: “What message did you receive?” The reply is: “I received one time; I do not know whether there were others.” This does not establish that the center offered only one time.

On the fictional staff card, authorized staff show a practice schedule with two offered times. The card contains no real record or identifier. Carole corrects the map: “two times on the practice schedule — verified in the card; one time received — reported by the resident; why only that time was received — to check.” She removes the arrow claiming that only one time was offered. The resident says: “That is what I meant about the message.” Confirming her own statement does not confirm everyone’s cause.

The fare branch remains open. Ask the resident on card B about costs that day; the schedule card cannot answer it. In real work, choose an appropriate person or authorized record under local access procedures and obtain only necessary information. Do not promise absolute secrecy, a new schedule or a service. Two branches remain, with sources and honest unknowns.

## [practice] Try it, then repeat

Put the missed visit at the center of a page. Draw two possible branches from cards A and B: childcare/timing and fare. Card C has no reply. For each arrow, write the source, reported/verified/to check and a person or authorized record that could help. Do not invent a reply for card C.

In a triad, play BHW, resident or staff, and observer. Each round takes two minutes for the first map and question, one for specific feedback, two for correction and retry, and one for recording and changing roles. Three rounds take 18 minutes so everyone plays BHW. Only the staff card verifies two times on the practice schedule; why one time was received is still unknown.

The observer points out one unsupported arrow. The BHW asks about the gap, removes or labels the arrow to check and keeps another explanation open. Peer feedback is practice, not a facilitator grade. Alone, draw using the cards, circle a gap, write the question, read the correction card and revise. Arrange later direct observation; watching or clicking the check cannot replace it.

## [check] Choose the next step

First look at the illustration of the discussion around the corrected map. In the dilemma, the group says at the fifth why that the mother lacks knowledge. Choose the next step and explain your reason before reading feedback.

After choosing, compare all three explanations and the corrected ending with your map. It should contain two branches with evidence or honest uncertainty and proportionate verification steps. This task is not diagnosis, an appointment or a service promise. The check supports reflection; the directly observed map, correction and retry provide competency evidence.

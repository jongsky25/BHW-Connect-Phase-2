## [scenario/core] Why is Liza quiet? {m6.gather}

Liza, 29, is speaking with BHW Mila for a household profile. A neighbor is beside her and she keeps looking at the door. She answers every question with “I’m fine.” Instead of guessing the missing details, Mila asks whether they can move somewhere more private and which language Liza prefers. She explains that relevant information is for the health team and service planning.

:::takeaway
Gathering information begins with a comfortable setting and a clear purpose.
:::

## [concept/core] Listen before giving advice {m6.empathy}

Mila sits at Liza’s level and puts her phone away. She waits for the story to finish: Liza feels ashamed about missing several health-center visits. Mila replies, “It sounds difficult to manage caregiving and getting there.” She does not call Liza lazy or promise to fix everything. A calm voice, appropriate eye contact and pauses leave space for a reply; do not force eye contact when it makes someone uncomfortable.

:::takeaway
Respectful listening gives the person room to describe the real concern.
:::

## [contrast/core] Open first, then clarify {m6.clarify}

“Why don’t you follow instructions?” sounds like an accusation. “What makes it hard to attend?” invites an explanation. Follow an open question with focused clarification: “When was your last visit?” Yes-or-no questions are useful when confirming one detail. Avoid asking three questions at once or putting the answer into the person’s mouth.

:::takeaway
Use open questions for the story and focused questions to clarify information.
:::
:::check
? Which opening question is most useful for Liza?
- “Did you miss it because you could not be bothered?”
+ “What got in the way of attending?”
- “You will go tomorrow, right?”
> It explores the barrier before deciding what caused it or how to solve it.
:::

## [concept/core] Reported, observed, and unconfirmed {m6.assess}

Liza says no one can stay with her two children. Mila notices the appointment card is unavailable, but that does not prove there was no appointment. In the notes, separate “Liza reports” from direct observations. Clarify an uncertain date; mark information “not yet confirmed” when a record still needs checking. Assessing information means checking its source and relevance, without assigning a diagnosis or judging the person.

:::takeaway
When assessing information, distinguish reports, observations and details still requiring verification.
:::
:::check

+ Date not yet confirmed; check the record.
- She never attended a checkup.
- She is not interested in her health.
> A missing document is an information gap, not proof of nonattendance.
:::

## [concept/core] Report enough for action {m6.record}

Mila uses the approved form: date, relevant concern, information source, action taken and needed follow-up. For example: “Liza reports difficulty finding childcare; last attendance date needs verification. Requests a conversation with the midwife about scheduling.” Send this to the appropriate health team member through an authorized channel. Keep names and private details out of public group chats. Confirm receipt and identify who will act next.

:::visual 1
:::takeaway
Recording and presenting information requires clarity, a next step and the appropriate recipient.
:::

## [practice/core] One message, then hear it back {m6.health-promotion}

Mila explains the agreed next step in plain language. “To check that I explained it clearly, how would you describe the plan when you get home?” If Liza’s response differs, Mila explains it another way and checks again. Teach-back checks the clarity of the explanation, not the person’s intelligence. Use a suitable picture if helpful; keep advice within training and the health team’s instructions.

:::takeaway
Check a clear message through the person’s own explanation, not a nod alone.
:::

:::

## [practice/core] Try the whole conversation

Work in pairs: one person plays Liza and the other the BHW. Begin with permission and an open question, hear the barrier, clarify one detail and summarize before suggesting action. Then write a three-sentence handoff for the midwife: the concern, confirmed and unconfirmed information, and the help requested. Switch roles. When studying alone, speak the lines aloud and write the report using fictional details only.

:::takeaway
Listening, assessment and recording form one chain: a guess can travel into the report.
:::

## [practice/standard] Household profiling with a purpose {m6.profile}

Before profiling, explain the form’s purpose and who will use it. Ask one question at a time, respect a person’s wish not to answer yet, and never guess a blank field. If someone speaks for Liza, give her the opportunity to respond. Summarize the record so she can correct errors. More personal information does not automatically make a better profile; collect what the authorized task requires.

:::takeaway
A profile needs a clear purpose, accurate answers and respect for the person.
:::

## [scenario/standard] A family-planning question {m6.family-planning}

Jo, 24, is worried about something she heard about family planning. Ask what specifically concerns her and listen without mocking the belief. A BHW can explain general information from approved materials, while choosing an appropriate method requires discussion with Jo and a midwife or qualified provider. Identify where she can get private counseling; do not decide for her.

:::takeaway
Family-planning communication supports the person’s choice and appropriate counseling.
:::

## [scenario/standard] When someone feels overwhelmed {m6.distress}

Ben, 37, says he has felt low for several weeks. A possible opening is, “Thank you for telling me. What feels hardest right now?” Listen privately and help him reach a health professional; avoid saying “just think happy thoughts” or assigning a diagnosis. If there is immediate danger to safety, follow the local emergency referral protocol at once. Do not promise to conceal danger from people who need to help.

:::takeaway
Listening offers support; diagnosis and clinical care belong with a qualified health professional.
:::

## [scenario/standard] Discuss smoking without blame {m6.smoking}

Ramon, 45, feels attacked when called “undisciplined.” First ask permission to discuss smoking. Explore what he would like to change and what barriers he anticipates. Summarize his response and offer a connection to health-center support. One agreed step is more useful than a long lecture he has stopped hearing. Medication and suitable treatment should be discussed with the provider.

:::takeaway
Planning improves when the person’s own goal and barriers are heard.
:::

## [contrast/standard] What was unheard in Rosario’s story {m6.barriers}

In the Rosario case studied in Module 7, poverty, service gaps and misinformation affect the family together. “Why didn’t you follow instructions?” is not enough. “What were you told, and what prevented you from getting help?” is more useful. Communication brings barriers into view and clarifies them. The next module examines which partners can address them.

:::takeaway
A barrier to services is important information, not a reason to judge a family.
:::

## [concept/standard] Adapt the message to the audience

Liza and the midwife need different information. Liza needs a plain next step and a contact. The midwife needs the concern, verified details and requested help. A barangay planning meeting needs aggregated barriers without identifying people. Choose a picture, spoken explanation or secure written report to fit the audience and purpose; do not turn a private case into a public story.

:::takeaway
The same concern can be presented differently for the purpose and appropriate recipient.
:::

## [practice/standard] Improve the handoff

Read this fictional handoff: “The family is difficult. They refuse to attend.” Replace it with a short factual report using Liza’s situation. Show what she said, what is verified and what is needed from the midwife. Do not invent numbers. Have a partner read it: can they identify the next step without guessing?

:::takeaway
A useful handoff supports action instead of labeling a person.
:::

## [practice/deep] Three roles, one feedback round

In groups of three, assign a BHW, a resident with a concern, and an observer. Rotate through profiling, family planning, emotional distress and smoking. The observer names one exact helpful phrase and one phrase to change. Repeat the difficult part before switching roles. Do not use a classmate’s personal experience as material without permission.

:::takeaway
Communication skills improve through specific feedback and another attempt.
:::

## [contrast/deep] When accounts differ

Liza says the appointment was canceled; a neighbor says she was not interested. Do not choose a story because one voice is louder. Ask Liza, check the authorized record and identify the source of each detail. If uncertainty remains, tell the health team. Respecting a person and being careful with evidence can happen together.

:::takeaway
When accounts differ, clarify and verify before deciding.
:::

## [practice/deep] Present at a meeting without names

In this exercise, three fictional households share a childcare barrier. Prepare a one-minute report without names or identifying details: what is the pattern, what information is missing, and what small service change could be discussed? End with a clear question for the group. Do not promise that a proposal is approved before the responsible people decide.

:::takeaway
A meeting presentation asks for a concrete decision while protecting private stories.
:::

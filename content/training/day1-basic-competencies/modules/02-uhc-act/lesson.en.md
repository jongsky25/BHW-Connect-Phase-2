## [scenario/core] Mang Ernesto's question

"Is it true consultations at the health center are free now? I only heard
it on the radio." That was Mang Ernesto's question to BHW Beth one
morning, while waiting in line.

Beth knew there was a new law — she had heard about it at a meeting the
month before. But honestly, she could not explain clearly what it had
actually changed. "Let me check and I'll get back to you," she answered —
an answer she honestly did not want to give again.

:::takeaway
When a law is not clear to the BHW, it is not clear either to the person
who is counting on them for it.
:::

## [concept/core] The goal of the UHC Act {m2.enactment, m2.philhealth}

Republic Act 11223, the Universal Health Care (UHC) Act of 2019, was
enacted in February 2019. Its goal: ensure that every Filipino has access
to affordable, quality health service whenever they need it — without
having to suffer hardship over the cost.

One of the biggest steps toward that: every Filipino is now
automatically included in PhilHealth, the National Health Insurance
Program. That does not make every service free. People with income, such
as employees and the self-employed, still pay their premium, and some
benefits can only be used after registering with an accredited provider.

:::takeaway
UHC's direction is clear: from a system that waits for someone to pay, to
one that guarantees service everyone can reach.
:::

## [concept/core] Four concrete changes {m2.outpatient, m2.primary-care-provider, m2.referral-system, m2.program-integration}

UHC is not just a promise on paper — four things change at once in how
an ordinary Filipino experiences asking for health help.

First, PhilHealth benefits now include **outpatient consultation**, not
only hospital confinement, for people registered with an accredited
provider. Second, every Filipino is to register with a **primary care
provider** of their choice — the first place to go, instead of whoever
happens to be available. Third, the law calls for a clearer, more
organized **referral system** between the barangay health station, the
health center, and the hospital, so a patient does not get lost in the
middle of the process. Fourth, the DOH brought its previously
separate health programs together into one system, instead of each
programme running on its own.

:::visual 1
:::takeaway
These four changes are connected — each one has to do with how a person
moves from having a need to actually receiving service.
:::

## [practice/core] Which of this is already happening for us?

Back to Mang Ernesto. Of the four changes you just learned, not all of
them arrive at the same pace in every barangay — some are already felt,
some are not yet.

Try it: think about your own barangay. Is everyone there already
automatically covered by PhilHealth? Do you know anyone registered with
a primary care provider? If someone needs a referral, is the process
clear, or is there still confusion about where to go?

It is not a failure if your answer to some of these is "not yet" — that
is exactly the first step: knowing what has actually arrived in your own
area before deciding what should happen next.

:::takeaway
Before proposing an improvement, first get clear on which changes have
actually reached your own barangay, and which have not.
:::

## [concept/standard] The health board and the BHW's part in moving it forward {m2.health-board, m2.bhw-skill, m2.health-promotion, m2.hepo-designation, m2.competency}

UHC also brings a new partner: the **provincial or city health board**,
tasked with helping organize and manage the health system at the
provincial or city level. Suggestions and concerns from the barangay can
reach it, through the midwife and the municipal or city health office.

Because of these changes, a higher level of skill is also expected of
BHWs, and health promotion within communities is intensified. This is
also why BHWs were designated HEPOs — you already learned this in
detail in Module 1.

So the skill this calls for is not only knowing the law: it also means
spotting the chance to improve how it is carried out, and getting that
suggestion to the right person — a fellow BHW, the midwife, all the way
up to the health board.

:::takeaway
UHC does not just change the system — it also expects the BHW to
contribute: observation, suggestion, and action.
:::

## [practice/standard] Propose it, and talk it through first

Now try connecting it all. Have you noticed something in your own
barangay that does not yet fully follow UHC's direction — for example,
it is still unclear who to approach before a referral, or a resident
does not yet know they are already a PhilHealth member?

Before acting on your own, talk it through with a fellow BHW or the
midwife first. There may be a reason things are still done that way, or
there may be another approach you had not thought of. The point is not
to immediately change everything — the point is to start the
conversation openly.

:::takeaway
A suggestion that was never discussed is unlikely to ever be carried
out — it needs to pass through discussion before it becomes action.
:::

## [practice/deep] The first small step

Think again about the gap you noticed. Instead of waiting for a big
change to come from above, what is one small, concrete thing you could
do starting next week?

For example: if you noticed residents don't yet know they're
automatically covered by PhilHealth, you could start with a short
reminder on your next household visit — it doesn't need to be a full
campaign right away, just a consistent mention until it becomes
something everyone knows.

Big changes in a system usually start with a small change in habit —
and the BHW who makes that change first, within their own reach, is the
one who speeds it up for everyone else.

:::takeaway
You don't need to wait for a big change from above — the small step you
start today is what speeds up the change's arrival in the barangay.
:::

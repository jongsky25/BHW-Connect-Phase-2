## [section-8] One purpose, clear teamwork

In this fictional situation, Malou and two fellow BHWs are preparing invitations for an agreed outreach activity. The purok and time are assigned, but it is not yet clear who will prepare the invitations or who will report completion. Saying everyone will help is not enough.

Good teamwork uses a shared purpose, clear roles, early communication, openness to feedback and respectful relationships. These help the team carry out its work for community health. Cooperation does not grant authority to make clinical decisions or share confidential information.

## [teamwork-five-practices] Five practices of a good team

First, clarify why you volunteered: to improve community health. Second, clarify each member’s role and responsibility. Third, discuss issues affecting the work early. Describing the effect on the task is enough; private reasons do not have to be disclosed.

Fourth, listen to feedback from colleagues and the community and use specific suggestions to improve the work. Fifth, build good relationships, including outside work, while respecting personal lives. Such social activities are voluntary. Nobody has to spend money, join a trip or take part in a close-contact physical game to prove they are a good teammate.

## [teamwork-role-agreement] Agree on the task

At the table, the group clarifies the task: prepare the invitations before ten o’clock. Malou prepares them, one colleague checks completeness and another reports to the designated contact. Each person repeats the task and time they accepted.

This is an example of sharing an already authorized task, not a universal rule for every barangay. A change to an official assignment must first be checked against the local process and permission from the designated supervisor. A peer BHW cannot assign work beyond a colleague’s training or authorization. Also clarify whom to contact if there is a difficulty.

## [teamwork-early-handoff] Raise issues early, hand over clearly

Before the deadline, a colleague says, “I cannot finish checking the invitations.” Malou responds, “Thank you for telling us early. What is done and what remains?” She does not demand private reasons or blame her colleague.

A handoff identifies what is done, what remains, the deadline and any permission still needed. Changes requiring permission are checked with the designated supervisor. The receiving person accepts and repeats the task; simply leaving a message is not enough. Share only necessary information with an authorized recipient through an appropriate channel. An immediate danger or serious breach is raised promptly with the appropriate contact under the local process; a peer discussion must not delay it.

## [teamwork-feedback-completion] Give feedback and confirm completion

After the task, Malou says, “Telling us early helped. Next time, let us write down who will report back.” Her feedback names an action and a suggestion, not a personality trait. She listens to her colleague’s explanation and asks what change would help.

The person who accepted the task reports, “The invitations were checked before ten o’clock.” The group confirms completeness against the agreement and informs the designated contact. If work remains, they record the next step, responsible person and follow-up time. A task is not complete merely because it was handed over. The group thanks one another and offers a voluntary chance to check in socially.

## [teamwork-application-check] Apply: a task will not be finished

At another fictional outreach activity, Malou’s colleague cannot finish the assigned preparation. There is no immediate danger, but an assignment needs to change. There is still time to contact the designated supervisor. The colleague does not want to share her personal reason.

Choose the best of the three responses. Explain how it shows clear roles, early communication and respectful teamwork. Then say who will accept the task, what permission must be checked and how completion will be confirmed. You do not need to watch the optional story to answer.
